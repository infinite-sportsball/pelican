Gullett Blanks A's in Game 4 as Morgan's 1st-Inning Dash Holds Up
The Reds got what they needed before Oakland had quite settled into its defensive crouch, before the light towers had softened from afternoon-white into the pale October dusk that pools over the East Bay flats around six o'clock. Joe Morgan, Cincinnati's second baseman, opened the game by pulling a 2-1 pitch from Ken Holtzman on a line into right-center, and two hitters later he rode Johnny Bench's ground single up the middle all the way home, rounding third without hesitation and daring the Oakland center fielder to beat him with a throw the man could not quite make. One run, tallied in the flick of a catcher's wrist and a runner's decision, and that was the sum of what Holtzman would surrender across eight innings that deserved kinder company.

What Don Gullett did with that lone tally over the next three hours belongs in the small permanent library of Series pitching lines. The Reds' twenty-one-year-old left-hander threw a complete-game four-hitter, struck out seven, walked exactly Joe Rudi, and needed one hundred and eighteen pitches to face thirty-three Oakland hitters, none of whom reached third base until it no longer mattered. He worked without visible strain, the way certain young pitchers do before they know enough to be tired, using the fifty-three-degree air and the wind cutting left to right across the Coliseum's synthetic outfield the way another man might use a following breeze on a golf course. Holtzman across the diamond was nearly his equal, but on this particular arrangement of the October evening the one run was the whole story.

The ninth turned briefly interesting. Matty Alou lined Gullett's first pitch through the left side and Rudi worked a four-pitch walk, and for a moment the Coliseum crowd, thinned by the cold but not yet resigned to it, remembered that this was still a ballgame. Sal Bando flied to Pete Rose in shallow left. Mike Epstein swung through a two-two pitch. Angel Mangual took strike three looking to end it, and Gullett walked off the mound with the ball in his glove and Bench trotting out to meet him for the professional handshake of a road team that had come to Oakland, taken what it needed in the first inning, and defended it for eight and a half more without ever letting on that the margin might have mattered.
