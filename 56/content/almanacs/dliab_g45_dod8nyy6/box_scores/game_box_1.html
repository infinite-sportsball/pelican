World Series to Dodgers
Casey Stengel waited for Don Larsen's 177th pitch before he came out of the dugout, and by then the Yankees were behind. They had led 5-0 after one inning. Joe Collins walked, Mickey Mantle sent a Sal Maglie pitch into the deep right-center grass and circled the bases for an inside-the-park home run, Gil McDougald singled in a third run, and Andy Carey's two-out double drove home two more. The crowd of 43,432 settled in for what looked like a coronation.

Brooklyn's answer arrived in pieces. Carl Furillo pulled a Larsen pitch down the left-field line in the second for a solo home run, and two innings later he was at it again, singling, taking third on Maglie's own line single, and scoring when Jim Gilliam tapped a slow roller back to the mound that Larsen lunged for and threw away. Yogi Berra's solo shot off Maglie in the seventh made it 6-2, and Sandy Amoros's two-run drive into the right-field seats in the eighth pulled it to 6-4. Larsen had struck out eleven Dodgers by then, working through an afternoon in which he was almost dominant and never quite in control, and he came back out for the ninth at 153 pitches.

Gilliam led off by doubling down the right-field line. Pee Wee Reese took four pitches, none of them close. Duke Snider bounced a single through the left side to load the bases with no one out, and Jackie Robinson worked the count full and walked, Gilliam trotting home to make it 6-5. The first boos came down out of the upper deck then - scattered, almost embarrassed, the sound Yankee crowds make when they don't yet want to believe what they are watching. Stengel did not move. Bob Cerv stayed sitting in the bullpen. Larsen faced Gil Hodges with the bases still loaded. Ball one. Ball two, low. Strike, called. Ball three. The boos thickened with each delivery, and on 3-1 Larsen threw his seventh walk of the afternoon and Reese walked home with the tying run, and the Stadium came down around him in the unmistakable Bronx register, the one that calls a man a bum to his face and means it. Amoros grounded into a force at the plate for the first out. Then Furillo, who would be named World Series MVP, dropped Larsen's 177th pitch into shallow right; Robinson scored from third, Hodges came around on a wave from his coach and beat the throw home, and Brooklyn led 8-6.

Now Stengel came. He climbed the dugout steps slowly, the old man's gait that conceded nothing, and made the long walk to the mound with one finger raised toward the bullpen, Cerv already trotting in. Larsen stood waiting, glove up over the lower half of his face, eyes fixed somewhere past Stengel's left shoulder, and the boos found a second target and rained down on his manager too, the fury Yankee crowds reserve for a hook that came an inning and three walks too late. Stengel took the ball without a word. Larsen walked off the mound looking at no one. Cerv struck out Roy Campanella, hit the Brooklyn reliever Randy Jackson with a pitch, and got Gilliam to ground out to second, and the booing did not stop until the Dodgers had taken the field. Jackson, who had relieved Maglie in the eighth, set the Yankees down in the bottom half - Collins grounded to first, Mantle lined out to center, Berra walked, Enos Slaughter popped to short - and the long October afternoon, four hours and one minute by the Stadium clock, emptied out into the upper Bronx. Larsen's line read 8.1 innings, ten hits, eight runs, seven walks, eleven strikeouts, and 177 pitches.
