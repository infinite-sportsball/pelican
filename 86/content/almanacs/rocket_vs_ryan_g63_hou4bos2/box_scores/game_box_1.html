Astros Win 1986 World Series
Bill Buckner came up with two out and nobody on in the third, a 34-year-old first baseman with his ankles wrapped with training tape like they were Christmas presents, and worked Nolan Ryan to 2-2. Then he put the next pitch 342 feet into the right-field seats, and it was 2-2. It was the sixth time in this loop Buckner had taken Ryan deep. Six separate Sunday nights, same box, same bad ankles, same Mets castoff on the mound in the rainbow stripes, and every time the scoreboard in center kept its bull in the barn and just changed a number.

Boston had gotten its first run without a hit. Ryan walked Dwight Evans and then Rich Gedman on four pitches, Dave Henderson bunted them over, and Spike Owen grounded one to second to score Evans. Houston answered right away off Roger Clemens: Glenn Davis doubled, Jose Cruz singled him in, Alan Ashby singled, and Craig Reynolds pushed Cruz across on a force play. In the fifth Denny Walling hit a two-out 2-0 pitch 343 feet to left, a foot farther than Buckner's, and this time the bull fired its pistols. In the sixth Kevin Bass singled, stole second, and scored on Cruz's single, beating Evans's throw home. Cruz had three hits. Clemens went seven innings, walked nobody, struck out the side in the seventh, and still lost. He was 0-for-2 at the plate with two strikeouts, and Don Baylor struck out batting for him.

After Buckner's home run Ryan didn't give up another hit. He went eight innings on 115 pitches, allowed two hits, and struck out nine, and Hal Lanier took the ball from him anyway and gave the ninth to Charlie Kerfeld, a big 23-year-old right-hander who wore a George Jetson T-shirt under his jersey, which in a building named for the space program was about right.

Kerfeld walked Buckner on four pitches, none of them close. Jim Rice, who had struck out three times against Ryan, worked a full count and bounced a single up the middle. Evans lined a 1-2 pitch through the left side, and Buckner, running from second on those ankles, got as far as third and stopped there. John McNamara left him in. Dave Stapleton sat on the bench. Bases loaded, nobody out, down two, with Rice on second as the tying run and Evans on first as the go-ahead. Forty-six thousand people stood up at once, and forty-six thousand spring-loaded seats snapped shut behind them like a round of applause for the wrong team.

Kerfeld struck out Gedman on four pitches. Then Henderson, a week removed from the home run off Donnie Moore that saved Boston's season in Anaheim, fouled off four pitches and struck out on a full count. Two out. Buckner leaned off third with his hands on his knees.

Spike Owen swung at the first pitch and hit it harder than anything Boston hit all night, a line drive at 106 miles an hour into deep right-center. For a second it looked like it would turn into a tie game. Billy Hatcher got a good jump, ran under it on the carpet, and caught it at a dead run.

Houston won 4-2, and the noise went up into the roof and came back down. Thirteen pitches with the bases loaded, and nobody had moved. Rice stood on second with his helmet still on. Buckner had been ninety feet from home for three batters. He stood on third a second longer than he needed to, looked out at Hatcher still jogging in with the ball, then turned and limped across the carpet to the visitors' dugout.
