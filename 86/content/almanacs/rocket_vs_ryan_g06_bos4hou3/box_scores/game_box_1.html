It's Over! Boston 1986 Defeats Houston 1986
Inside the Astrodome, where the light stayed flat and the big electronic bull waited for Houston to give it something to shoot about, the Boston Red Sox and Houston Astros played the kind of game two hard throwers produce when neither one is interested in going home early. Houston's Nolan Ryan worked eight innings and struck out eight on 126 pitches. Roger Clemens gave Boston eight innings of his own, allowing four hits and striking out six on 108 pitches. Clemens also struck out in all three of his plate appearances, including one failed bunt that required three separate attempts, which is why pitchers generally hire other people to hit.

Houston grabbed a 2-1 lead in the first when Billy Hatcher drove home Bill Doran with a triple and scored on Denny Walling's groundout. Ryan held it there until the sixth, when Bill Buckner, who had been hitting big-league fastballs since his Dodgers debut in 1969, took his first pitch 404 feet to tie the game. Across eighty-six versions of this Sunday, Buckner hit eight home runs. Six came off Ryan. There are six nights in which a 34-year-old man with wrapped ankles looked at one of the hardest-throwing pitchers alive and decided this was manageable work. Because Buckner played for Boston, the bull did not fire its pistols. The line score merely changed.

Boston kept going. Jim Rice singled, Dwight Evans doubled, and catcher Rich Gedman sent an 0-1 pitch through the right side at 100 miles per hour to score Rice for a 3-2 lead. In the eighth, Boston center fielder Dave Henderson added what became the winning run, driving Ryan's first pitch 401 feet to left. Hatcher answered in the bottom half with his third hit, a 387-foot homer off Clemens, and this time the bull, cowboys, spaceships, flags and pistols got their forty seconds. Houston was within 4-3, and the sealed building sent the noise back onto the field.

Boston reliever Bob Stanley took the ninth and threw eight pitches, all strikes. Houston right fielder Kevin Bass singled with one out, but left fielder Jose Cruz grounded the next pitch into a 5-4-3 double play. Buckner received the final throw at first, the Boston bench came up making noise, and the pocket of Red Sox fans in the visitor sections answered them. Sixty-eight years remained unresolved, because this was only Game 2, but Buckner's homer and Stanley's eight strikes had moved Boston one game closer.
